Today, almost all the quarks in our universe are regularly confined within larger particles known as hadrons. However, during the first few microseconds after the Big Bang, the universe was filled with an extremely hot and dense state of matter known as quark–gluon plasma (QGP), in which quarks and gluons could move freely rather than being confined inside hadrons.

Recently, the CMS Collaboration at CERN's Large Hadron Collider (LHC) has reported the direct observation of a wake-like structure produced by energetic particles as they travel through quark–gluon plasma. This observation provides new insight into how energetic particles interact with this extreme state of matter.

According to Olga Evdokimov of the University of Illinois Chicago, the result offers an opportunity to study how energetic particles transfer their energy and momentum to the quark–gluon plasma.

When high-energy quarks or gluons are produced in particle collisions, they can form collimated sprays of particles known as **jets**. As these energetic partons travel through the QGP, they interact with the surrounding medium and transfer some of their energy and momentum to it. These interactions can disturb the plasma, producing a wake-like pattern along the particles' trajectories.

Earlier studies using events involving a Z boson accompanied by a jet had provided preliminary indications of this phenomenon. However, the new analysis uses pairs of jets produced in opposite directions to isolate the wake signal more effectively.

The new measurement reveals a distinct reduction in the number of particles behind the trajectory of a jet. This pattern is consistent with the expected characteristics of a wake generated as an energetic parton moves through the quark–gluon plasma.
