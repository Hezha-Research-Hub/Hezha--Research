## What Are Gravitational Waves?

Imagine throwing a stone into water and watching ripples spread outward from the point of impact. According to general relativity, extremely massive and accelerating objects, such as two black holes orbiting each other, can generate waves in the structure of spacetime. These waves are known as **gravitational waves**.

Gravitational waves travel through space at the speed of light. As they pass through Earth, they produce incredibly small changes in the distances between objects.

## How Are They Produced?

One of the most important sources of gravitational waves is the merger of two black holes.

As these two massive objects gradually approach each other, they lose energy in the form of gravitational waves and eventually merge. During this process, a portion of their mass is converted into energy and emitted as gravitational radiation.

## How Are They Detected?

Detectors such as **LIGO** use laser interferometers to measure extremely small changes in the lengths of two perpendicular arms.

As a gravitational wave passes through a detector, it causes these arms to stretch and contract alternately. These tiny variations can be recorded through changes in the interference pattern of the laser beams.

In 2015, the LIGO detectors directly observed gravitational waves produced by the merger of two black holes for the first time. This discovery was one of the most important experimental confirmations of general relativity and marked the beginning of a new era in astrophysics.

## Scientific Significance

Gravitational waves provide an independent way of observing and studying the universe. They allow scientists to investigate objects such as black holes and neutron stars, as well as test general relativity under extremely strong gravitational fields.

Interestingly, scientists can convert the signals of gravitational waves into sound. This is why they are sometimes described as the "sound of black hole collisions."
